August 12, 2026 was a day when the same headline economic data produced two contradictory market reactions.

## The relief rally

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics released the July Consumer Price Index that morning, showing headline inflation at 3.4% year-over-year and core CPI (ex food and energy) at 2.5%, both a tenth of a point below June and in line with consensus forecasts, according to CNBC's coverage of the report. Energy prices fell another 1.5% on the month. Wall Street read the numbers as reducing the odds of a Federal Reserve rate hike at the September meeting — Bloomberg reported futures markets pricing in less than a 40% chance of a hike, while CNBC cited a roughly 64% chance the Fed holds rates steady at 3.50%–3.75%. The S&P 500 pushed to a record close and the Nasdaq 100 rose over 1%, per Bloomberg's markets wrap.

## The bond and gold market's quieter warning

Not every corner of the market shared the enthusiasm. A Bloomberg newsletter published that same day carried the headline "Bonds, Dollar and Gold Are Flashing an Inflation Warning" — a signal that, even as equity traders cheered, fixed-income and precious-metals investors remained wary of longer-run inflation and fiscal risk. That skepticism had a concrete source: the Treasury's 10-year note auction a day earlier drew the highest financing cost for that maturity since 2007, and a 30-year bond auction due the following day was expected to price at the highest yield since 2001, a reflection of how much more investors are demanding to fund the widening federal deficit.

## Bitcoin and oil moved to a different rhythm entirely

Crypto markets ignored the equity rally altogether. Japanese exchange bitbank's daily commentary for August 12 was headlined (translated) "BTC continues to decline, watching U.S. CPI and the Middle East situation," noting bitcoin had fallen for a second straight session, changing hands around ¥10.13 million after dipping toward ¥10.08 million. The drag wasn't monetary policy — it was the Strait of Hormuz, which has remained effectively closed to Western-linked shipping since the U.S.-Israel-Iran war escalated in February, keeping Brent crude elevated near $81 and unsettling risk sentiment in digital assets. In Tokyo, the Nikkei 225 gained a comparatively modest 255 points in afternoon trading, per Minkabu, as investors there waited on the U.S. CPI print before committing further, while the yen slipped slightly to roughly ¥159.37 per dollar.

## Why the split happened

The mechanism connecting these threads is that two separate risk factors were being priced simultaneously on August 12. Cooling CPI eased a purely monetary-policy risk — the chance the Fed tightens further — which is what equities trade on most directly. But it did nothing to address the other two risks weighing on bonds, gold, and crypto: a supply-driven oil shock from the still-unresolved Hormuz closure, and a fiscal-financing problem visible in Treasury auction yields. Stocks could rally on Fed relief while gold, bonds, and bitcoin continued pricing a geopolitical and deficit-driven risk premium that a single soft CPI reading couldn't touch.

In my view — and this is interpretation, not confirmed fact — this divergence is worth watching into the fall. If the Strait of Hormuz standoff drags on and Treasury yields keep climbing on deficit concerns, the "good CPI, calm stocks" narrative could prove fragile, since equities have historically struggled to keep rallying once real long-term borrowing costs rise much further from here.
