## In my view

August 10 was the day the AI buildout's financing question came into focus: it's no longer just about how much capital gets spent on chips and data centers, but about who is lending it and on what terms. Intel chose equity — a dilutive but comparatively clean $15 billion (upsized to roughly $20 billion) stock sale — while the same day's reporting on Oracle's blown-out credit-default swaps showed the debt side of the AI trade is where real investor anxiety is concentrating. My read is that more capex-heavy AI names will follow Intel toward equity issuance over the next few quarters, accepting near-term dilution because credit markets are becoming the more expensive, more skittish way to fund the buildout. Layered on top, a still-unresolved Strait of Hormuz standoff and a surprisingly soft July jobs report pulled sentiment in opposite directions — cheap-money optimism supporting risk assets even as expensive oil threatened the disinflation story that cheap money depends on.

## The evidence

Intel announced a $15 billion underwritten common stock offering on August 10 — its first public share sale since the company's 1971 IPO — to fund chip capacity tied to AI, physical AI, and advanced packaging demand, according to Intel's own announcement and Bloomberg's coverage. CNBC reported the deal was upsized to roughly $20 billion, priced at $95 a share; shares fell as much as 5% in premarket trading before recovering, with Motley Fool estimating dilution at around 3% to 3.4%.

The same day, Bloomberg's markets newsletter, headlined "AIndicators Hint at Doubts in Credit Markets," drew a parallel to Jay Cooke's 19th-century railroad financing collapse to argue that transformational technology built on borrowed money carries real financing risk before it generates returns. The piece noted Oracle's credit-default swap spreads had hit a multi-year high, and cited a Bank of America fund-manager survey in which 34% of respondents named AI hyperscaler capital spending as the most likely source of a future systemic credit event — double the share who said so in April.

Elsewhere, the same AI enthusiasm that's straining credit markets was lifting equities in Tokyo: the Nikkei 225 rebounded 2.08% to close at 66,970.22, snapping a three-session slide, as Minkabu and Kabutan both attributed the move to strength in U.S. semiconductor names and a weaker-than-expected July U.S. jobs report — nonfarm payrolls down 23,000 with wage growth slowing to 3.2% year-over-year — that cooled expectations for near-term Fed tightening. Bitcoin traded above $65,000, up from a roughly $64,849 open, benefiting from the same soft-jobs, rate-cut-friendly mood.

U.S. equities, by contrast, barely moved: the S&P 500 slipped 0.04% to 7,754.54, the Dow eased 0.1%, and the Nasdaq lost 0.3%, per Yahoo Finance and Washington Post market wraps. The drag came from oil, which jumped roughly 5% as Iran and Oman remained short of a deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz — Iran's foreign minister said Tehran wanted sanctions relief and war reparations before allowing traffic to resume, CNBC reported — reviving inflation concerns just as soft labor data was supposed to ease them.

Taken together, August 10 looked less like one story than three intersecting ones: a financing bifurcation within the AI trade itself, a labor-market wobble that's bullish for risk assets, and a geopolitical oil shock that complicates the very disinflation narrative those risk assets are leaning on.
